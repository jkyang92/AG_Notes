\section{Lecture 7 (Jason McCullough): Constructing the resolution and examples}\label{lec:7-HHL-construction}
This section is an introduction to the papers \cite{BCHSY} and \cite{BH25}.

As in Section~\ref{sec:*****}, $X = X(\Sigma)$ denotes a simplicial toric variety where $\Sigma$ is a fan with $n$ rays, and we set $M = N^{*}$. We will often make use of the exact sequence:
$$
0\to M \xrightarrow {\alpha} \ZZ^{n} \xrightarrow {deg} \Cl(X) \to 0
$$
We write $S = k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]= k[\NN^{n}]$ for the Cox ring of $X$. As such, $S$ comes equipped with an``irrelevant ideal", which is of great importance, because it is what distinguishes $X$ from other
toric varieties that have the same graded ring as Cox ring. However, for the purposes of this and the next few sections, this ideal is truly irrelevant---it will play no role.

The inclusion of one toric variety in another is defined by 

A  prime ideal generated by elements $x^m-x^p \in S := k[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, where $m,p\in\NN^n$ are multi-indices, in the 
Cox ring of $X$, defines a toric subvariety.  Any toric subvariety $Y\subset X$ may be defined in this way if the inclusion is
induced by a homomorphism from the dense torus of $Y$ to that of $X$ whose image is a subtorus.
By \cite{Eisenbud-Sturmfels}, primeness implies both that
the set $L = \{m-p\mid x^m-x^p\in I \cdot k[\ZZ^n]\}$ is a saturated lattice and  that there is a one-to-one correspondence between pure binomial ideals that are prime and saturated lattices meeting
 $\NN^n$ only in 0. Such binomial ideals correspond to affine, not necessarily normal, toric varieties in $\AA^{n}$. 
 The inclusion of dense tori corresponds to the quotient map $M \to M/L$ of character latices.
 
 The ring $R \ce S/I$ is isomorphic to the semigroup ring $k[\NN^n/L]$, where $\NN^n/L$ is the image of $\NN^n$ under the quotient map $\ZZ^n\rightarrow \ZZ^n/L$.
 If $L = M $ then $I$ is the ideal corresponding to the subvariety consisting of the identity element of the dense torus. This example will be treated in the next section.


Theorem 3.4 of \cite{BCHSY} provides a canonical  resolution $\FF$ of the normalization $\overline R$
of the ring $R$. It is obtained from a $\ZZ^{n}$-graded, $L$-equivariant $S$-free resolution $\tF$ of 
an $S$-module $M_L\subset k(x_{1}, \dots, x_{n})$ (Definition~\ref{def:M_{L}}) by factoring out a regular sequence.
\david{The choice of notation $M_L$ is poor, since it has nothing to do with the central player $M$! Is it too late to change this??}
The relation of $\FF$ to $\tF$ is analogous to the relation of a space to its universal cover.

Although the free modules in $\tF$ are not finitely generated, the quotient $\FF$
is a finite, $S$-free, $\ZZ^{n}/L$-graded resolution of length equal to the 
codimension of $I$, reproving the theorem of Hochster that
$\overline R$ is Cohen-Macaulay. Moreover, the free modules $\FF_{i}$ have degrees
corresponding to the Bondal-Thomsen classes defined in the previous section. It may be easiest to
understand the following general definitions while reviewing the special case treated in Example~\ref{first example of the HHL resolution}.


The resolution $\tF$ is built from an infinite $L$-invariant cellular subdivision of $L_\RR \ce \RR\otimes L$
and a stratification of $L_{\RR}$.  Although \cite{BCHSY} treats a more general stratification, we will
treat only the case of  the ``ceiling stratification'' and the $L$-invariant subdivision of $L_\RR$ defined by the coordinate hyperplanes $H_{i,j}\ce \{x\in L_\RR\mid x_i = j\}\quad j\in \ZZ$. This divides $L_\RR$ into cells $A$ whose closures are convex polyhedra, meeting along their faces; see Figure~\ref{fig1}.

%% , and the resulting $S$-free complex $\tilde{\FF}$ is $L$-graded and acquires through this the structure of an $S[L]$-module. The generators of the free modules in $\tilde \FF$ correspond to
%% chambers in this cell complex, and the $i$-th term of the complex $\tilde\FF$ is $\oplus S\cdot\sigma$, where
%% the direct sum runs over all cells of dimension $i$.

We regard the subdivision above as making $L_{\RR}$ into an infinite cell complex.
If $A$ is a cell, and $p = (p_1,\dots, p_n) \in \RR^n$ is a point in the
interior of $A$, then we give $A$ the weight \jay{If we are going to use $\ZZ^n$ for the weights can we switch to $\psi$ for consistency with \cite{BCHSY}?}
$$
\Psi(A) \ce \lceil p \rceil \in \ZZ^{n}.
$$
This is independent of the choice of $p$ because the subdivision of $L_\RR$ is induced by the coordinate hyperplanes.
 
If $B$ is 
a cell in the boundary of the closure $\overline A$, then $B$ is the intersection of
$\overline A$ with some of the hyperplanes $H_{j}$. Thus any interior point of $B$ will
be a limit of points in the interior of $A$ at which some coordinates approach an integer. It follows that
$\Psi(B)$ will be termwise less than or equal to $\Psi(A)$; in fact $\Psi(A) - \Psi(B)$ is a vector
of 0s and 1s.

\begin{defn}[The HHL Complex]\label{def of F}
 The term of $\tF$ corresponding to a cell $A$ is defined to be $S(-\Psi(A))$, with  
 \[
    \tF_i=\bigoplus_{\dim A=i}S(-\Psi(A)).
   \]
Thus if $p$ is an interior point of $A$
we have
\[
-\deg(\Psi(A)) = -\deg( \lceil {p}\rceil) = -\sum_{i=1}^n\lceil p_i\rceil D_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\lfloor -p_i\rfloor D_i,
\] 
which is a \BT class.

 We define the differential of the resolution $\tF$ by choosing
an orientation on $L_\RR$ and homogenizing, according to the weights assigned to the chambers, the usual differential of
a cell complex $\partial_{L_{\RR}}A = \sum\epsilon_{i}B_{i}$, where each $\epsilon_{i} = \pm 1$.
\jay{If we are only going to do the regular case ($\epsilon_i=\pm 1$) should we just replace all of the $\epsilon_i$ with $\pm$?}
\end{defn}

With this homogenization, the component of the differential that maps $S(-\Psi(A))$ to $S(-\Psi(B_{i}))$ is multiplication by $\epsilon_{i}x^{\Psi(A) - \Psi(B_{i})}$, where
$\epsilon_{i} = \pm 1$, depending on the chosen orientations. The
differential preserves the $\ZZ^{n}$-grading, the complex $\tF$ is $L$-equivariant.



In particular,
$$
\tF_{0} = \bigoplus_{p \text{ a vertex of the cell complex }L_{\RR}}S(-\Psi(p)),
$$
so there is a natural augmentation map from $\tF_{0}$ to a module $M_{L}$ generated by the corresponding monomials:

\begin{defn}\label{def:M_{L}}
 $M_{L}\subset k[\ZZ^{n}]\subset k(x_{1}, \dots, x_{n})$ is the $S$-submodule generated by the monomials
 $$
 \{x^{\Psi(p)} \mid p \hbox{ is a vertex of the cell complex }L_{\RR}\}.
 $$
\end{defn}


In Theorem~\ref{thm:HHL-resolution} we will prove that the complex $\tF$ is a resolution of $M_L$.

\begin{remark}
 Because $\Psi(A)$ is termwise $\geq \Psi(B)$ when $B$ is a vertex of $A$, the module $M_{L}$ contains (and thus is generated by) the monomials $x^{\Psi(\mathbf{p})}$ for all points $\mathbf{p}\in L_{\RR}$. Thus, while the resolution $\tF$ depends on the cell structure, the module $M_L$ only depends on the lattice $L$.
\end{remark}

Because the differentials of $\tF$ are $L$-invariant, we can form a quotient by the action of $L$, obtaining the $\ZZ^{n}/L$-graded complex $\FF$. Proposition 3.3 of~\cite{BCHSY} shows  that the quotient of $M_L$ by this $L$-action is $\overline{R}$, regarded as a fractional ideal of $R$. Putting these results together shows that  $\FF$ is a resolution of $\overline R$.

We now describe these resolutions in the two simplest cases: when $I = (x_1-x_2)$, so that $\overline R = R$, and when $I = (x_1^3-x_2^2)$, so that $\overline R = k[x_1,x_2/x_1]$. 
To simplify the notation, we write $S =k[x,y]$ in place of $k[x_1,x_2]$.  

\begin{example}$R \ce k[x,y]/(x-y)$:\label{first example of the HHL resolution}
In this case the lattice $L$ is generated by the single vector $ (1,-1)\in \ZZ^2$,
which corresponds to the monomial $x/y$ if we regard $k[\ZZ^{2}]$ as a subset of $k(x,y)$.
Since the cellular subdivision of $L_\RR$ is $L$-invariant, it suffices to consider its restriction to  a fundamental domain for the action of $L$, which is contained
in a single square of the lattice $\ZZ^2$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig1}. In this special case, the construction is the same as that of the ``hull resolution'' of \cite[Section 3]{Bayer-Sturmfels} (see also \cite[Sections 9.2--9.3]{MScomb}) of $R$.

The set $L_{\RR}\subset \RR^2$ meets this fundamental domain in the diagonal line $A$ (the dot represents a general point of $A$, which gives the label of $A$). There are infinitely many 0-cells, which are $L$-translates of $a$, and infinitely many 1-cells, which are the $L$-translates of $A$. We choose an $L$-invariant orientation, and take the differential to be the usual one for a cell complex, homogenized by taking the upper left corner $a$  
to have weight $x^0y^0=1$, the lower right corner $b$ to have weight $x^1y^{-1}$, and 
 the cell $A$ to have weight given by the interior point  $p= (1/4, -1/4)$, so that $A$ has weight
$ \Psi(p) = (\lceil 1/4\rceil,\lceil -1/4\rceil) =(1,0)$. With these choices $\tF$ and its augmentation to $M_{L}$ have the form
$$
\tF := 0 \to 
\tF_{1} \xrightarrow{\tilde d(A)} 
\tF_{0} \rightarrow 
M_{L} = \sum_{m\in \ZZ} S\cdot (x^{m}/y^{m}) \rightarrow 0,
$$
where
$$
\tF_{i} = \begin{cases}
  \oplus_{\ell \in L} S(-Psi(\ell(A)))&\hbox{ if } i = 1\\
\oplus_{\ell \in L} S(-Psi(\ell(a)))&\hbox{ if } i = 0\\
\end{cases}
$$
 If we also write $\ell(A)$ for the generator of $S(\ell(A))$, then the differential $\tilde d$ may be written as
$\tilde d(\ell(A)) = x\,\ell(a)-y\,\ell(b)$,  
which makes sense because $b$ is the translate of $a$ by a generator of $L$.   
Taking the quotient by 
$L$ we obtain the usual two-term resolution $\FF: S(-1)\xrightarrow{d} S$, with differential $d = (x-y)$.

If we give $x$ and $y$ the natural weights $2$ and 3, then $d'$ is $\ZZ^2/L \cong \ZZ$-homogeneous as a map $S(-4) \oplus S(-3) \to S\oplus S(-1)$.

Taking the quotient of $\tF$ by $L$ is equivalent to setting the action of a generator $x^{3}/y^{2}$ of $L$ to 1, that
is, to forming $\tF/(1-x^{3}/y^{2}).$

Since $M_L$ is $\ZZ^{2}$-graded, and the elements 1 and $x^{3}/y^{2}$ have
different $\ZZ^{2}$-degrees, the element $1-x^{3}/y^{2}$ is a nonzerodivisor on $M_L$, and thus
the quotient operation preserves exactness.

The determinant of $d$ or $d'$ is 
$(x^3-y^2)$, so the cokernel is annihilated by this element.
%but the cokernel is not $k[x,y]/(x^3-y^2)$!
Since $M_L$ is generated by
the monomials $x^{\Psi(p)}$, as $p$ runs over the vertices of $L_\RR$, $M_L/(1-(x^3/y^2))M_L$
is generated by the monomials $x^{\Psi(p)}$ for $p$ in the fundamental domain. 
These are the fractions $1,x,x^2/y$, which generate
the fractional ideal $(1, x^2/y) = (1,t)  = \overline R$.

Moreover, since $x^3/y = y^2/y = y$ in $R$, we have
$$
(1, x^2/y)*d' = 0
$$
and we see that the cokernel of $d'$ is also isomorphic to 
$R\cdot(x^2/y, y) = R\cdot (x^2/y)$, as required.

\section{Lecture 8 (Ryan Watson): More examples and features}\label{lec:8-HHL-examples}

The previous lecture focused on the construction of the resolution, and through examples illustrated that for a toric subvariety $X'\subseteq X$ with corresponding lattice $L\subseteq M$, the resolution constructed is a $\ZZ^{n}/L$-graded resolution of the normalization $\overline{R}$ of the ring $S/I_L$. The proof of this is deferred to Section~\ref{lec:9-HHL-proof}. In this section, we describe in more detail the structure of the resolution as it relates to the Bondal-Thomsen classes. We start with the following summary of the resolution and a general statement about its relationship with the Bondal-Thomsen collection.

\begin{cor}
  \label{cor:hhlSummandsBT}
  Let $X'\subseteq X$ be a toric subvariety,  let $L\subseteq M$ be the corresponding sublattice, and let $I_{L}$ be the corresponding binomial ideal. There is an $S$-free $\ZZ^{n}/L$-graded resolution $\FF$ of the
  normalization $\overline R$ of the ring $S/I_{L}$ whose free modules are given by $\FF_{i} = \oplus S(-\deg\Psi(A))$, where the sum is over all cells $A$ of $(L \otimes \RR)/L$ having dimension $i$. Moreover, each $S(-\deg\Psi(A))$ is a \BT class. 
  The construction depends only on the Cox ring with its grading, and not on the additional data of the
  irrelevant ideal of the toric variety.
\end{cor}

Here is an important special case: 

\begin{cor}\label{cor:diagonalUniform}
If $X$ and $X'$ are toric varieties with the same Cox ring $S$, then the HHL complex of free $S\otimes_k S$-modules resolving the closed graph of the identity $X\subseteq X\times X$ is equal to the complex associated with the 
graph $X\subseteq X\times X'$ of the birational map from $X$ to $X'$ that restricts to the identity on the dense torus.
Moreover, the summands of the free modules will all be of the form $S(d)\boxtimes S(d')$ for certain pairs of Bondal-Thomsen classes $d,d'$. 
 \end{cor}
\begin{proof}
Let $T$ be the common dense torus of $X$ and $X'$. The ideal of the diagonal in $T\times T$ is
generated by the binomials 
$$
\{x^{a}\otimes 1-1\otimes x^{a}  \mid a\in M\} \subset k[x_{1}^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
\otimes k[x_{1}^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
$$
which corresponds to the sublattice 
$$
L = \{(v,-v)\mid v\in M\}\subset M\times M.
$$
 This is
the same in both cases of the corollary.
\end{proof}

Not every pair of \BT classes will appear in the resolutions described in Corollary~\ref{cor:diagonalUniform}.
Nevertheless, the following statement holds:
\begin{cor}\label{cor:diagonal-BTcollection}
Every \BT class appears both as the first and as the second member of a pair $(d,d')$ that appears in the HHL resolution of the diagonal of $X$. \end{cor}
\begin{proof}
The lattice 
\[
L := \{(\mathbf{v},-\mathbf{v}) \in \ZZ^{2n} \,|\, \mathbf{v}\in M_X\}.
\] 
in the proof above projects surjectively onto each factor, and the cell decomposition of $L_{\RR}$
induced by the embedding into $\ZZ^{n}\times \ZZ^{n}$ is the same as that induced by the embedding into
$\ZZ^{n}$.
  
  Thus the line bundles that appear in the resolution of the diagonal are of the form $\cO_{X\times X}(-\lceil (\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p})\rceil)$ for $\mathbf{p}$ a point in $\RR \otimes M_X$ which can then be expanded as follows:
  \[\cO_{X\times X}(-\lceil (\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p})\rceil)=\cO_X(-\lceil \mathbf{p}\rceil)\boxtimes \cO_X(\lfloor \mathbf{p}\rfloor).\]
  The left hand side of this summand is $\cO_X(D)$ for some $D$ in the BT collection by the same reason as in Corollary~\ref{cor:hhlSummandsBT}. And $\cO_X(\lfloor \mathbf{p}\rfloor)$ is quite directly the generator of the BT collection associated to $\mathbf{p}$. Finally, as $\mathbf{p}$ varies over all points in $\RR \otimes M_X$, every Bondal-Thomsen element appears as the Bondal-Thomsen collection was defined as the image of $M_X$ under the map described in Definition~\ref{defn:BTcollection}.
\end{proof}

The Fourier-Mukai transform with kernel equal to a resolution of the diagonal,  a key tool in the proof of the main theorem of \cite{BES}, takes a sheaf $\cF$ to a monad for $\cF$ (that is, a complex whose only homology is $\cF$).  We will see \david{where?} that the terms of this complex are direct sums of sheaves
 drawn from the sheafified left-hand factors in the HHL resolution of the diagonal. Consequently, applying the Fourier-Mukai transform to arbitrary coherent sheaves yields monads whose summands are all Bondal-Thomsen elements, as follows:

\begin{cor}
  If $\mathcal{F}$ is a coherent sheaf on $X$, then it is the homology of a monad whose summands are all Bondal-Thomsen elements of $X$.\qed
\end{cor}

 The HHL resolution of the diagonal is minimal as a complex of free $S$-modules. This does not hold for many other $L$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem} \cite{HHL}\label{thm:HHL-resolution}
With notation as in Section 7, the complexes $\tF$ and $\FF$ are $S$-free resolutions of $M_{L}$ and $\overline{S/I_{L}}$, respectively. 
\end{theorem}

The original proof of the Hanlon-Hicks-Lazarev Theorem in~\cite{HHL} is fairly technical.  However, two recent preprints have dramatically simplified the proof.  The preprint~\cite{BCHSY} (see in particular Proposition 3.2) relates the Hanlon-Hicks-Lazarev construction to a more classical algebraic construction involving binomial ideals, and provides an elementary and purely algebraic proof of the main result, as well as a broad generalization of this result.  The preprint~\cite{BH25} utilizes a similar basic idea as~\cite{BCHSY}, but provides a terser and narrower proof.

\[

\]

$\tF_0$ are the same as those of $M_L$, so there is a natural augmentation $\tF_0\to M_L$, and it is easy to check that this composes to 0 with the differential of $\tF$.

$$
S = S[L]/(\langle z^{\mathbf{u}}-1 \,|\, \mathbf{u}\in L\rangle).
$$
If $P$ is any $L$-graded $S[L]$-module, and $0 \neq \mathbf{u}\in L$, then $1-z^{\mathbf{u}}$ is a nonzerodivisor on $P$ because $p$ and $z^{\mathbf{u}}p$ have distinct degrees. It follows easily by induction that if $\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_r\in L$ is a basis, then the sequence $z^{\mathbf{u}_1}-1,\ldots,z^{\mathbf{u}_r}-1$ is a regular sequence on $S[L]$ and also on the module $M_L$. $$
This is a $\ZZ^n/L$-graded complex of finitely generated free $S$-modules.

  Building on Example~\ref{ex:cuspidal-cubic}, where $n=2$ and $L=\ZZ\cdot (3,-2)$, we illustrate the proof that $M_L \otimes_{S[L]} S \cong \overline{S/I}$.

  We can  indentify $\ZZ^2/L$ with $\ZZ$ by the isomorphism $\gamma:(a,b)\mapsto 2a+3b$.  With this identification, the semigroup $A$ is the generated by $2$ and $3$ in $\ZZ$ and it's saturation $\overline{A}$ is $\NN$. Moreover, $\NN$ is generated as a module over $A$ by the  element $1$.

To prove that $1$ is in the image of $M_L$, consider the vertex $d=(2,-4/3)$ of the induced cell structure in Figure~\ref{fig:cuspidal cubic 1}. The Laurent monomial associated to this point by $\Psi$ is $x^2y^{-1}$.
  This corresponds to the vector $(2,-1)$, and $\gamma(2,-1)=1$, and so we get the generator of the semigroup in this way.
  
  To follow the steps of the proof given above observe that $(2,-1)-(2,\frac{-4}{3})=(0,\frac{1}{3})$. This tells us that
  \[\gamma(2,-1) = 0\cdot \gamma(1,0) + \frac{1}{3}\cdot \gamma(0,1)=\frac{1}{3}\cdot 3 = 1.\]

  The reverse direction is more subtle. Starting with the same generator $1$ of $\overline{A}$, we work backwards. As there is not in general a unique vertex of $L_{\RR}$ which realizes this generator, there will be choices involved.
  First we choose some way of presenting $1$ in terms of $2$ and $3$ with $\RR_{\geq 0}$ coefficients, for example $1=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2$.
 Following the notation from the proof, $\mathbf{q}=(\frac{1}{2},0)$. Let us still choose $\bm{u}=(2,-1)$.
  The goal now is to find a point $\bm{p}$ such that $\lceil \bm{p} \rceil$ is componentwise $\leq (2,-1)$.
  The proof suggests the following difference:
  \[\bm{p}=\bm{u}-\bm{q} = (\frac{3}{2},-1).\]
  As $\lceil \bm{p} \rceil = (2,-1)$, we exactly recover $(2,-1)$, but the more interesting part of the proof is to realize that $\bm{p}\in L_{\RR}$. Here this follows as $\bm{p}=\frac{1}{2}(3,-2)$.

  In this case, the point $\bm{p}$ is exactly the vertex $c$ from Figure~\ref{fig:cuspidal cubic 1}. Moreover, as we vary the choice of $\bm{q}$, we get all points on the line segment from $c$ to $d$ in Figure~\ref{fig:cuspidal cubic 1}. \jay{Consider reproducing the figure with the segment highlighted}
\end{example}
