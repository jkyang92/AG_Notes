\subsection{What is a derived category?}
Let $X$ be a variety over a field $\kk$, and let $\coh X$ denote the abelian category of coherent sheaves on $X$. The central object of these notes is the \defi{bounded derived category} $\Db(X)$.\footnote{Technical note: since we will never consider unbounded derived categories in these notes, we can write $\Db(X)$ instead of $D^b(X)$ or various other notations found in the literature.}  As noted above, the category $\Db(X)$ naturally ``contains'' all homological information carried by $\coh X$, but it turns out that $\Db(X)$ often has better properties. We briefly recall its construction and refer to \cite{Huybrechts2006} for a thorough treatment.

Let $\mathcal E^\bullet$ and $\mathcal F^\bullet$ be bounded complexes of coherent sheaves on $X$.  As a reminder, this means that
\[
\mathcal E^\bullet = \cdots \cE^i \overset{\phi^i}{\to} \cE^{i+1}\overset{\phi^{i+1}}{\to} \cdots
\]
is a complex such that each $\cE^i$ is a coherent sheaf, and where $\cE^i=0$ for all but finitely many values of $i$.  Let $f\colon \cE^\bullet\to \cF^\bullet$ be a morphism of complexes.  This naturally induces morphisms
\[
H^i f\colon H^i(\cE^\bullet) \to H^i(\cF^\bullet)
\]
for all $i$, where $H^i(\cE^\bullet):= \ker \phi^i /\im \phi^{i-1}$.  We say that $f$ is a \defi{quasi-isomorphism} if each of these maps $H^if$ is an isomorphism of sheaves on $X$.


\begin{defn}
  The \defi{bounded derived category of $X$} has objects given by bounded complexes of coherent sheaves and morphisms given by chain maps modulo homotopy, with quasi-isomorphisms formally inverted.
  %% Let $K^b(X)$ denote the \defi{bounded homotopy category} of $\coh X$: its objects are bounded cochain complexes of coherent sheaves on $X$, and its morphisms are chain maps modulo homotopy. The \defi{bounded derived category} $\Db(X)$ has the same objects as $K^b(X)$, but every quasi-isomorphism is formally inverted.
\end{defn}

\begin{remark}
We are aiming to include as little discussion of the technical aspects of derived categories as possible, but there is a rich world here to be explored.  
For instance, by definition, a morphism is a finite ``zig-zag diagram'' of genuine morphisms of complexes and formally inverted quasi-isomorphisms.  It turns out that a simpler description works: any morphism in the derived category $\begin{tikzcd} \cE \arrow[r] & \cF \end{tikzcd}$ in $\Db(X)$
can be represented by a \defi{roof} 
$\begin{tikzcd} \cE & \arrow["\simeq"', l] \cE' \arrow[r] & \cF \end{tikzcd}$ 
in which the left arrow is a quasi-isomorphism.
See~\cite{Caldararu05} or \cite{Huybrechts2006} for excellent expository introductions.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:roofPP2}
  On $\PP^2$, there are no nonzero maps $\cO\to\cO(-3)$ of sheaves, since $\HH^0(\PP^2,\cO(-3)) = 0$.  However, the Koszul complex
\[\begin{tikzcd}
  \cO & {\bigl[\,\cO(-1)^3} & {\cO(-2)^3} & {\cO(-3)\,\bigr]}
  \arrow["\simeq"', from=1-2, to=1-1]
  \arrow[from=1-3, to=1-2]
  \arrow[from=1-4, to=1-3]
  \end{tikzcd}
  \]
  is exact as a complex of sheaves on $\PP^2$.  Thus the Koszul complex induces a quasi-isomorphism between the sheaf $\cO$ and the complex $[\cO(-1)^3 \gets \cO(-2)^3\gets \cO(-3)]$.  The derived category allows us to formally invert this quasi-isomorphism. There is an honest morphism of complexes from $[\cO(-1)^3 \gets \cO(-2)^3\gets \cO(-3)]$ to the complex $\cO(-3)[2]$, which consists of $\cO(-3)$ in cohomological position $-2$. Specifically, that morphism simply sends $\cO(-3)$ to $\cO(-3)$ by the identity and sends the other sheaves to $0$. Combining these, we obtain a morphism in the derived category, from $\cO$ to $\cO(-3)[2]$:

  \arrow[from=1-3, to=1-2]
  \arrow[from=1-4, to=1-3]
  \arrow["\pi", from=1-4, to=1-5]
  \end{tikzcd}\]

Passing to the derived category destroys the abelian structure: kernels and cokernels no longer make sense, and there are no short exact sequences. What survives is the  structure of a \defi{triangulated category}, in which short exact sequences are replaced by \defi{distinguished triangles}.

\begin{defn}
  A \defi{triangulated category} is an additive category $\cD$ equipped with an autoequivalence $E\mapsto E[1]$ (the \defi{shift}) and a class of distinguished triangles $E\to F\to G\to E[1]$ subject to a list of axioms (see \cite[\S1.2]{Huybrechts2006}). Moreover, every morphism $f\colon E\to F$ fits into a distinguished triangle
  \[\begin{tikzcd}
    E \rar{f} & F \rar & \cone(f) \rar & E[1].
  \end{tikzcd}\]
\end{defn}

The category $\Db(X)$ is triangulated, with the shift given on a complex $\cE^\bullet$ by $\cE[1]^i=\cE^{i+1}$ and $d_{\cE[1]}=-d_{\cE}$, and with $\cone(f)^i=\cE^{i+1}\oplus \cF^i$ for a chain map $f\colon\cE^\bullet\to\cF^\bullet$. A full subcategory closed under shifts and cones is a \defi{triangulated subcategory}; it is \defi{thick} if it is moreover closed under direct summands. The smallest thick triangulated subcategory containing a set of objects $\{E_i\}$ is the subcategory they \defi{generate}, and we say $\{E_i\}$ \defi{generates} $\cD$ if this subcategory is all of $\cD$. We say a functor between triangulated categories that preserves shifts and distinguished triangles is \defi{exact}. 

Now we come to a key point:  there is a fully faithful embedding $\coh X\into \Db(X)$ sending a sheaf $\cE$ to the complex consisting only of $\cE$ in cohomological degree $0$:
\[
\cE \mapsto [\cdots\to  0 \to \cE \to  0\to \cdots].
\]
What ``fully faithful'' means is that morphisms are preserved.  More is true, as \emph{derived} morphisms are also preserved.  That is, if you take two sheaves $\cE$ and $\cF$, then we have a natural isomorphism:
\[ \Ext^i_X(\cE,\cF)\cong \Hom_{\Db(X)}(\cE,\cF[i]). \]
In other words, the $\Ext$ groups from the category of sheaves are realized as precisely the morphisms in the derived category, with $\Ext^i$ corresponding to morphisms from $\cE$ to the shift $\cF[i]$.
%derived morphisms into shifts.

    In big picture terms, one thing that this means is that there is no loss of information when we pass from $\coh X$ to $\Db(X)$.  So if we can understand properties about $\Db(X)$, then this will tell us properties about the thing we originally cared about, which was $\coh X$.  If $\Db(X)$ is easier to describe than $\coh X$---which is the case in many examples---then this provides motivation for studying $\Db(X)$ instead of $\coh X$.

We henceforth identify a sheaf with the corresponding complex so that a short exact sequence $0 \to \cE \to \cF \to \cG \to 0$ in $\coh X$ is represented by a distinguished triangle $\cE \to \cF \to \cG \to \cE[1]$ in $\Db(X)$, but note that distinguished triangles allow extensions between objects that live in different cohomological degrees, thus not every distinguished triangle comes from a short exact sequence.

\subsection{Fourier--Mukai transforms}
Any object of the derived category of a product $X\times Y$ induces an exact functor $\Db(X)\to \Db(Y)$.  The resulting functors play a major role in the study of sheaves on varieties, and will come up throughout these notes.
%in a manner formally analogous to integral transforms in functional analysis.


Most standard functors of sheaves from $\coh X$ to $\coh Y$ will induce a corresponding (total) derived functor from $\Db(X)$ to $\Db(Y)$.  For instance, if $f\colon X\to Y$ is a proper morphism, then $f_*\colon \coh X\to \coh Y$ is left exact, and properness guarantees both that $f_*$ preserves coherence and that the derived functor takes bounded complexes to bounded complexes, so that there is a corresponding functor $Rf_*\colon \Db(X)\to \Db(Y)$.  We will not give a full review of derived functors, again referring to standard references for those unfamiliar with this theory.

Fourier--Mukai transforms are built as compositions of standard derived functors.

\begin{defn}\label{defn:FMtransform}
  Let $X$ and $Y$ be smooth projective varieties, with projections
  \[\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small, row sep=small]
    & X\times Y \arrow[dl,"p"'] \arrow[dr,"q"] & \\
    X & & Y.
  \end{tikzcd}\]
  The \defi{Fourier--Mukai transform} with \defi{kernel} $\cK\in \Db(X\times Y)$ is the exact functor
  \[ \Phi_\cK\colon \Db(X) \xto{Lp^*} \Db(X \times Y) \xto{-\Lotimes\cK} \Db(X \times Y) \xto{Rq_*} \Db(Y), \]
  where $Lp^*$, $-\Lotimes\cK$, and $Rq_*$ are the derived functors induced by $p^*$, $-\otimes\cK$, and $q_*$, respectively, but since $p$ is flat $Lp^*$ is the usual pullback.
\end{defn}

\begin{remark}
    Those seeing this definition for the first time may have the impression that Fourier--Mukai transforms are somewhat technical, and that it would be difficult to work out an explicit example in practice.  This is correct!  It is easy to write out a formal definition of a Fourier--Mukai transform, and these functors do have nice properties.  But in practice, these functors can be very subtle, and it can be hard to understand how to compute even seemingly simple examples.  This will be a key theme in later sections.  In fact, perhaps the most technical lemma in the entire notes amounts to computing the Fourier--Mukai transforms of a finite set of line bundles.  (See Lecture~\ref{lec:14-proof-outline}.)
\end{remark}


\begin{remark}
    A theorem of Orlov (which is quite interesting, but will play no role in these notes) asserts that, for smooth projective $X$ and $Y$, every exact equivalence $\Db(X)\xto{\simeq}\Db(Y)$ arises as a Fourier--Mukai transform for some kernel $\cK$, which is unique up to isomorphism in the derived category \cite{Orlov97}.
\end{remark}

By far the most important case of the Fourier--Mukai transform occurs when $Y = X$ and $\cK \in \Db(X\times X)$ is the structure sheaf $\cO_\Delta\ce\iota_*\cO_X$ of the diagonal subscheme $\iota\colon\Delta\to X\times X$\!.  In this case we have:

\begin{prop}\label{prop:diagonalidentity}
  The functor $\Phi_{\cO_\Delta}\colon \Db(X)\to \Db(X)$ is isomorphic to the identity functor.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
  Write $\iota$ for the diagonal embedding, so that $p\circ\iota=q\circ\iota=\id_X$. Using the projection formula, and the isomorphism $L\iota^*\circ Lp^*\cong L(p\circ\iota)^*=\id$ (note that $\iota$ is not flat, so the derived pullback $L\iota^*$ is essential here), we compute
  \[   \Phi_{\cO_\Delta}(\cE)=Rq_*\!\left(p^*\cE\Lotimes \iota_*\cO_X\right)\cong Rq_*\iota_*\!\left(L\iota^*p^*\cE\right)\cong R(q\circ\iota)_*\,L(p\circ\iota)^*\cE\cong \cE. \qedhere   \]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Beilinson's resolution of the diagonal}
Because we are working in the derived category, the sheaf $\cO_{\Delta}$ is isomorphic to any resolution of that sheaf.  Proposition~\ref{prop:diagonalidentity} can therefore become a powerful computational tool if we can replace $\cO_\Delta$ by an explicit and simple locally free resolution. In this case, the left derived tensor product with a complex of locally free sheaves will become the usual tensor product, allowing for more concrete applications.

On $X=\PP^n$, exactly such a resolution was constructed by Beilinson \cite{Beilinson78}. Let $\cT_{\PP^n}$ denote the tangent bundle and set
\[ \mathcal W\ce \cO_{\PP^n}(1)\boxtimes \cT_{\PP^n}(-1), \]
a vector bundle of rank $n$ on $\PP^n\times\PP^n$, where $\cF\boxtimes\cG\ce p^*\cF\otimes q^*\cG$ denotes the external tensor product.  There is a canonical section $s\in \HH^0(\PP^n\times\PP^n,\mathcal W)$ whose vanishing locus is precisely the diagonal $\Delta$ (see \cite[\S3]{Caldararu05} or \cite[\S8.3]{Huybrechts2006}).
%\mahrud{good opportunity for an enlightning M2 computation of the actual section here}
Since $\PP^n\times\PP^n$ is smooth, hence Cohen--Macaulay, and the vanishing locus $\Delta$ has codimension $n=\rank\mathcal W$, the section $s$ is regular, so the Koszul complex on $s$ is a locally free resolution
\[ 0\to \textstyle\bigwedge^n\mathcal W^\vee \to \cdots\to \bigwedge^2\mathcal W^\vee\to \mathcal W^\vee \to \cO_{\PP^n\times\PP^n}\to \cO_\Delta\to 0. \]
Using $\mathcal W^\vee\cong \cO(-1)\boxtimes \Omega^1_{\PP^n}(1)$ and $\bigwedge^i\bigl(\Omega^1(1)\bigr)\cong \Omega^i(i)$, we obtain $\bigwedge^i\mathcal W^\vee \cong \cO(-i)\boxtimes \Omega^i_{\PP^n}(i)$, so Beilinson's resolution takes the form
\[ \cO_\Delta \simeq \Big[\, \cO(-n)\boxtimes\Omega^n(n)\to \cdots\to \cO(-1)\boxtimes\Omega^1(1)\to \cO\boxtimes\cO\,\Big], \]
a complex each of whose terms is a box product of a line bundle with a twisted exterior power of the cotangent bundle. In the next lecture we combine this resolution with Proposition~\ref{prop:diagonalidentity} to prove that the line bundles $\cO(-n),\cO(-n+1),\dots,\cO$ generate $\Db(\PP^n)$. The same circle of ideas---now applied to resolutions of the diagonal on toric varieties---reappears in Lecture~\ref{sec:FMTransform} as the Fourier--Mukai transforms at the heart of the Cox category.

One reason that we work with derived categories instead of the category of coherent sheaves is that derived categories often allow for more elegant global descriptions and thus cleaner structural insights into properties of sheaves.
Most commonly, this is done by decomposing the derived category into simple building blocks, in a manner akin to finding a basis for a vector space.
%or writing an integer as a product of primes.

% To give examples of this, we must first outline the language
% In this lecture we decompose the derived category $\Db(X)$ of a smooth projective variety into simple building blocks.
To make this decomposition precise, we need to introduce the notions of a semiorthogonal decomposition and, in the best case, a full strong exceptional collection. See \cite[\S1.4]{Huybrechts2006}, \cite[\S3]{Caldararu05}, or \cite{Kuznetsov14} for more details.

Throughout this discussion, $\cD$ denotes a $\kk$-linear triangulated category, the case of interest being $\cD = \Db(X)$ for a smooth projective variety $X$.  Given a full triangulated subcategory $\cB\subseteq \cD$, its \defi{right orthogonal} $\cB^\perp$ is the full subcategory of objects $C$ with $\Hom_\cD(B, C)=0$ for all $B\in\cB$; the \defi{left orthogonal} ${}^\perp\cB$ is defined symmetrically. A sequence $(\cB_0,\dots,\cB_r)$ of triangulated subcategories is \defi{semiorthogonal} if $\cB_j\subseteq \cB_i^\perp$ whenever $j<i$.

\begin{defn}
  A \defi{semiorthogonal decomposition} $\cD = \langle \cB_0,\dots,\cB_r\rangle$ is a semiorthogonal sequence of subcategories that together generate $\cD$. That is, the smallest thick triangulated subcategory containing all objects from every $\cB_i$ is precisely $\cD$.
\end{defn}

We should think of this as akin to writing a vector space as a direct sum of subspaces.  The best possible scenario would be to have a basis for the vector space, and the corresponding notion for a derived category is the finest such decomposition, which comes from exceptional objects.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:SODfiltration}
  Semiorthogonal decompositions are frequently defined by a different condition, namely that every object $T\in\cD$ should admit a filtration
  \[ 0 = T_{r+1}\to T_r\to\cdots\to T_1\to T_0 = T \qquad\text{with}\quad \cone(T_{i+1}\to T_i)\in\cB_i. \]
  Semiorthogonality forces such a filtration to be unique up to isomorphism and functorial in $T$, and the objects admitting one form a thick subcategory.  When each $\cB_i$ is admissible, this condition is equivalent to the generation condition in the definition above (see \cite[\S2]{Kuznetsov14}). In particular, a semiorthogonal decomposition comes with projection functors $\cD\to\cB_i$ sending $T$ to $\cone(T_{i+1}\to T_i)$; these are what make semiorthogonal decompositions usable in practice.
\end{remark}

\begin{defn}
  %Let $\cD$ be a $\kk$-linear triangulated category. 
  An object $E\in D(X)$ is \defi{exceptional} if
  \[ \Hom_{D(X)}(E,E[\ell]) = \begin{cases}\kk & \ell=0, \\ 0 & \ell\ne 0.\end{cases} \]
  Since $\Hom_{D(X)}(E,E[\ell]) = \Ext^\ell_{X}(E,E)$, this is equivalent to saying that $\Hom_X(E,E)=\kk$ and all higher $\Ext$ groups vanish.
  An ordered sequence $E_0,\dots,E_r$ of exceptional objects is an \defi{exceptional collection} if $\Ext_X^\bullet(E_i,E_j)=0$ whenever $i>j$. It is \defi{strong} if in addition $\Ext_X^\ell(E_i,E_j)=0$ for all $i\le j$ and all $\ell\ne0$ (for a collection of sheaves the negative $\Ext$ groups vanish automatically, so only $\ell>0$ needs to be checked), and \defi{full} if the collection generates $\cD$. We will abbreviate ``full strong exceptional collection" to ``FSEC" throughout.
\end{defn}
\begin{example}
    Let $X$ be any projective toric variety (we will discuss background on toric varieties in Lectures~\ref{lec:4-toric-cox} and \ref{lec:5-toric-cox}). Any line bundle $L$ on $X$ is exceptional.  This is because:
    \[
    \Hom_X(L,L) = H^0(X,\cO_X) =\kk,
    \]
    since $X$ is projective and integral.  And we have
    \[
    \Ext^{\ell}_X(L,L) = H^{\ell}(X,\cO_X)=0 \qquad\text{for all }\ell>0,
    \]
    by Demazure Vanishing (see Theorem~\ref{thm:demazure} below) applied to the trivial divisor.
\end{example}

\begin{cor}\label{cor:UniquenessAndFSEC}
    For every sheaf $\mathcal F$ on $\PP^n$, there is a (unique up to homotopy) complex $F$ that is quasisomorphic to $\mathcal F$ and where $F_i = \bigoplus_{j=0}^n \cO(-j)^{m_{ij}}$.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
Let $K$ denote the homotopy category of complexes of graded $S$-modules whose terms are direct sums of line bundles in the Beilinson collection. There is a functor $K \to D^b(\PP^n)$ that sends $S(i)$ to~$\OO(i)$ for $-n \le i \le 0$. Fully faithfulness (resp. essential surjectivity) of this functor follows from the strong exceptionality (resp. fullness) of the Beilinson collection.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  Corollary~\ref{cor:UniquenessAndFSEC} demonstrates what we mean about an ``elegant global description''.  Any object in the derived category of coherent sheaves on $\PP^n$ can be identified with a minimal free complex $F_\bullet$ where each $F_i$ is a direct sum of $S(-n), S(-n+1), \cdots, S$, and that identification is unique up to isomorphism.  So while $\Db(\PP^n)$ may seem like a highly technical category, the corollary shows that it is in fact equivalent to something very concrete.

  Theorem~\ref{thm:beilinsonFSEC} and Corollary~\ref{cor:UniquenessAndFSEC} provide a model for what we want from a global description of a derived category.  By finding an FSEC of line bundles for $\Db(\PP^n)$, we have broken $\Db(\PP^n)$ into individual pieces, the $\cO(-i)$, each of which we totally understand; this allows us to provide essentially canonical representations for any sheaf on $\PP^n$, or more generally for any object in $\Db(\PP^n)$, in terms of the very simple objects $\cO(-n), \dots, \cO$ or, for the more algebraically minded, in terms of $S(-n), \dots, S$.

  A common metaphor is that finding an FSEC for a derived category is like finding a basis for a vector space.  From an FSEC, one gets essentially unique representations of all objects as in Corollary~\ref{cor:UniquenessAndFSEC}, just like how a basis provides unique representations of vectors.  The metaphor works on many levels:  bases are non-unique, and FSEC's can be as well.  Some bases are better than other: for instance, the monomials provide an especially nice basis for the vector space of degree $d$ polynomials in $n$ variables.  Similarly, an FSEC consisting of simple objects like line bundles is especially desirable.
\end{remark}

Another application of finding an FSEC is that we get an especially nice way to think about the Grothendieck group.  Recall that the \defi{Grothendieck group} $K_0(X)$ of a smooth projective variety $X$ is the abelian group 
%generated by the isomorphism classes $[E]$ of objects $E\in\Db(X)$, subject to the relation $[F]=[E]+[G]$ for every distinguished triangle $E\to F\to G\to E[1]$; equivalently, it is 
generated by the classes of coherent sheaves modulo the relations $[\cF]=[\cE]+[\cG]$ for every short exact sequence $0\to\cE\to\cF\to\cG\to 0$. The existence of a full exceptional collection has a striking consequence for this group.

\begin{cor}\label{cor:K0PPn}
  The Grothendieck group of $\PP^n$ is $K_0(\PP^n) \cong \ZZ^{n+1}$ with basis $[\cO(-n)],[\cO(-n+1)],\dots,[\cO]$.
\end{cor}
\begin{proof}
  The classes of the objects in a full exceptional collection generate $K_0$, since the collection generates $\Db(\PP^n)$. Semiorthogonality makes the matrix of the Euler pairing
  \[ \chi(E_i,E_j) = \sum_\ell (-1)^\ell \dim_\kk\Ext_X^\ell(E_i,E_j) \]
  upper triangular with $1$'s on the diagonal, hence invertible over $\ZZ$. Therefore the classes are linearly independent.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    As an immediate corollary, for every sheaf $\cF$ on $\PP^n$, the class $[\cF]$ in $K_0(\PP^n)$ can be uniquely written as a combination of the classes $[\cO(-n)], [\cO(-n+1)], \dots, [\cO]$.  Corollary~\ref{cor:UniquenessAndFSEC} can be viewed as a categorification of this fact about $K_0(\PP^n)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The BGG correspondence}

The correspondence is realized by an equivalence $R \co \Db(S) \xra{\simeq} \Db(E)$ of bounded derived categories of graded modules: let us explain how this equivalence works. Let $E^*$ denote the $\kk$-dual of~$E$, which is isomorphic as an $E$-module to $E(-n-1)$. For a graded $S$-module $M$, let $R(M)$ be the complex of free graded $E$-modules with
  \[   R(M)^i =  E^*(-i) \otimes_\kk M_{i} \quad\text{in cohomological degree } i, \]
  with differential given by multiplication by $\sum_{j = 0}^n e_j \otimes x_j$. 
 
  
  One extends $R$ from modules to complexes by applying the above formula term-wise and totalizing. The inverse of $R$, which we denote by $L$, is given as follows. For a graded $E$-module $P$, let $L(P)$ be the complex of free graded $S$-modules with
  \[ L(P)^i = S(i) \otimes_\kk P_{-i} \quad\text{in cohomological degree } i, \]
  whose differential is also multiplication by $\sum_{j = 0}^n x_j \otimes e_j$. One extends $L$ to complexes in the same way as $R$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:Rfunctor}
  Say $S = \k[x_0, x_1]$ and $M = S / (x_0)$. Since $\dim_\k M_i = 1$ for $i \ge 0$, each nonzero term of $R(M)$ has rank 1. And since $x_0$ annihilates $M$, the differential is just multiplication by $e_1$:
  $$
  R(M) = \quad \cdots \xla{e_1} E^*(-2) \xla{e_1} E^*(-1) \xla{e_1} E^*.
  $$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  Let $S$ be as in Example~\ref{ex:Rfunctor}, but let us now compute $R(S)$. Since $\dim_\k S_i = i +1$ for $i \ge 0$, we have $\rank R(S)^{i} = i+1$ for $i \ge 0$: notice how the Hilbert function of $S$ gets encoded as the Betti numbers of $R(S)$. The complex $R(S)$ looks as follows:
  $$
  R(S) = \quad \cdots \from E^*(-2)^3\xla{\begin{pmatrix} e_0 & 0 \\ e_1 & e_0 \\ 0 & e_1\end{pmatrix}} E^*(-1)^2 \xla{\begin{pmatrix} e_0 \\ e_1 \end{pmatrix}}  E^*.
  $$
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:BGGresidue}
  Since $\kk$ is concentrated in internal degree $0$, the complex $R(\kk)$ has a single nonzero term, the free module $E^*$ in cohomological degree $0$. Now to apply $L$, note that the graded piece $(E^*)_p =\bigwedge^p W$ contributes the term $S(-p) \otimes_\kk\bigwedge^p W$ in cohomological degree $-p$, and $e_j$ acts on $E^*$ by contraction, so the differential coming from $\sum_{i = 0}^n x_i\otimes e_i$ assembles these terms into the Koszul complex 
  \[  L(E^*)\colon\quad S\from S\otimes\bigwedge^1 W\from \cdots\from S\otimes\textstyle\bigwedge^{n+1}W,  \]
  sitting in cohomological degrees $-n-1, \dots, 0$, which is the minimal free resolution of $\kk$ over $S$. Hence $LR(\kk)\simeq \kk$ in $\Db(S)$, as Theorem~\ref{thm:BGG} predicts. The role of the twist in $E^*\cong E(-n-1)$ is that applying $L$ to the untwisted module $E$ instead yields the dual Koszul complex involving the $\bigwedge^p V$, whose only cohomology is $\kk(n+1)$ in cohomological degree $n+1$.

  Of course, the Koszul complex, which is a free resolution of $\kk$ over $S$, can be viewed as an exterior algebra.  So one can construct a natural isomorphism $\kk \cong L(E^*)\cong S\otimes_{\kk} E$ in $D(S)$ (ignoring grading twists). Similarly, we have $\k \cong RL(\k) = R(S) = S \otimes_\k E$ in $D(E)$ (once again ignoring gradings). That is, the injective resolution of $\kk$ over $E$ is naturally isomorphic to a symmetric algebra over $E$! This is a key idea behind Koszul duality; see e.g. \cite{PP05} for more.
\end{example}

Notice that $\rank_E R(M) = \dim_\k M$, and similarly for $L$. Thus, applying $L$ to any finitely generated~$E$-module gives a bounded complex. But since finitely generated $S$-modules are rarely finitely dimensional over $\k$, we typically have $R(M)_i \ne 0$ for $i \ll 0$. Nevertheless, if $M$ is finitely generated, the homology of $R(M)$ is as well, by the following lemma: 

\begin{lemma}[\cite{EFS}*{Proposition 2.3(a)}]
\label{lem:tor}
For any graded $S$-module $M$, we have $H_i(R(M))_j \cong \Tor^S_{i+j}(M, \k)_j$.
\end{lemma}

In particular, the functor $R$ sends finitely generated $S$-modules to the \emph{bounded} derived category of $E$. We include a sketch of a proof, since we will need this lemma again in Lecture~\ref{lec:18-tate-resolutions-PPn}.


\begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
The idea is that, forgetting gradings, $R(M)$ is isomorphic to $M \otimes_S K$, where $K$ is the Koszul complex on the variables in $S$. The proof thus boils down to keeping careful track of how this isomorphism interacts with the cohomological and internal gradings; we omit the details. 
\end{proof}

We omit the proof of the following theorem; a complete argument is more than one lecture allows. 


\begin{thm}[\cite{EFS}*{Corollary 2.7}, \cite{BGG}]\label{thm:BGG}
  The functors $L$ and $R$ descend to mutually inverse equivalences of triangulated categories
  \[  R\colon \Db(S)\xto{\simeq}\Db(E), \qquad L\colon \Db(E)\xto{\simeq}\Db(S). \]
\end{thm}


%A subtlety is hidden in the statement: for a finitely generated $S$-module $M$ not supported in $\m$, infinitely many graded pieces $M_i$ are nonzero, so $R(M)$ is an unbounded complex. However, $R(M)$ is exact in all sufficiently large cohomological degrees (specifically, in degrees beyond the Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity of $M$; see \cite[\S2]{EFS}), so it is quasi-isomorphic to a bounded complex and determines an object of $\Db(E)$; no such issue arises for $L$, since $P$ is finite-dimensional over $\kk$. 

Let us illustrate the equivalence by evaluating the composition at the residue field $\kk = S/\m$, where $\m = (x_0,\dots,x_n)$.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:BGGregularity}
  For a finitely generated graded $S$-module $M$, the complex $\mathbf R(M)$ is exact in cohomological degrees $i > \operatorname{reg} M$, and conversely the Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity of $M$ can be detected on the exterior side by applying $\mathbf R$ to the truncations $M_{\ge d}$ \cite[\S2]{EFS}. Regularity is usually defined through the vanishing of graded Betti numbers over $S$; under the BGG correspondence it becomes literal cohomological vanishing over $E$. This reformulation powers the Tate resolutions of Lecture~\ref{lec:18-tate-resolutions-PPn}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The singularity category of $E$}

The equivalence of Theorem~\ref{thm:BGG} interacts with two natural subcategories, and passing to quotients recovers $\Db(\PP^n)$. Let $\Db_\m(S)\subseteq \Db(S)$ denote the full subcategory given by complexes whose homology is finite dimensional over $\kk$, and let $\Perf(E)$ be the full subcategory of \emph{perfect complexes}, i.e. objects that are quasi-isomorphic to a bounded complex of finitely generated free modules. Since $\Db_\m(S)$ (resp. $\Perf(E)$) is the thick subcategory generated by twists of $\kk$ (resp. $E$), and the functor $R$ maps twists of $\kk$ to twists (and cohomological shifts) of $E$, one concludes:

\begin{prop}[\cite{EFS03}*{\S2}]\label{prop:BGGsupport}
  Under the equivalence of Theorem~\ref{thm:BGG}, the subcategory $\Db_\m(S)$ corresponds to $\Perf(E)$.
\end{prop}

%In the context of Example~\ref{ex:BGGresidue}, since the residue field is supported at $\m$, we have $\kk\in\Db_\m(S)$, and the proposition states that $R(\kk) = E^*$ is a perfect complex.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:BGGfiniteness}
  Proposition~\ref{prop:BGGsupport} exchanges two finiteness conditions that live on opposite sides of the correspondence. Over $S$, which has finite global dimension, every object of $\Db(S)$ is a perfect complex, whereas having finite-length homology is a strong restriction. Over $E$ the situation is reversed: every object of $\Db(E)$ has finite-dimensional homology, since $E$ itself is finite-dimensional, whereas perfection is a strong restriction because $E$ has infinite global dimension. For instance, the residue field $\kk$ of $E$ has an infinite minimal free resolution. This asymmetry explains why the same Verdier quotient construction below produces a geometric category on the symmetric side and a singularity category on the exterior side.
\end{remark}

\begin{defn}[see {\cite[Ch.~10]{Weibel1994}}]
  Given a thick subcategory $\cB\subseteq \cD$, the \defi{Verdier quotient} $\cD/\cB$ is the triangulated category with the same objects as $\cD$, obtained by formally inverting all morphisms whose cone lies in $\cB$.
\end{defn}

Applying this to $\Db_\m(S)$ recovers the geometry of $\PP^n$: there is an equivalence
\[ \Db(S)\,/\,\Db_\m(S) \simeq \Db(\PP^n), \]
which is the derived-category analogue of Serre's description of coherent sheaves on $\Proj S$ as finitely generated graded $S$-modules modulo those of finite length (compare Lecture~\ref{lec:5-toric-cox}, where the analogous statement $\Db(X)\simeq \Db(S)/\Db_B(S)$ is discussed for toric $X$).

On the exterior side, the analogous quotient is the \defi{singularity category}
\[ D_{sg}(E) \ce \Db(E)\,/\,\Perf(E), \]
in the sense of Buchweitz and Orlov \cite{Buchweitz2021,Orlov09b}; since $E$ is self-injective, $\Dsg(E)$ is equivalent to the stable module category of $E$. Combining Theorem~\ref{thm:BGG} with Proposition~\ref{prop:BGGsupport} and passing to Verdier quotients, we conclude:
\[ \Db(\PP^n) \simeq \Db(S)/\Db_\m(S) \simeq \Db(E)/\Perf(E) = D_{sg}(E). \]
That is, the BGG correspondence identifies the derived category of $\PP^n$ with the singularity category of the exterior algebra $E$. We will return to this equivalence in Lecture~\ref{lec:18-tate-resolutions-PPn} and discuss its generalization to toric varieties in Lecture~\ref{lec:19-tate-resolutions-toric}.
